Is speaking in tongues biblical?

Is the gift of tongues from God? Is it what most people today understand it to be? This book takes the question back to its source: what the Bible actually describes when it speaks of tongues, where the gift first appeared, what purpose it served, and what rules the apostles gave for its use, then follows twenty centuries of church history to explain how the modern phenomenon arose. This is not a book against the gifts of the Holy Spirit. God has not retired: He still heals, still gives preachers and teachers, still distributes every gift of 1 Corinthians 12 as He wills. The book asks one question: what does Scripture say the gift of tongues is?

What the Bible describes

At Pentecost in Acts 2, unlearned Galileans spoke real human languages (Parthian, Median, Elamite, Latin, Arabic and more) verified in real time by native speakers. The Greek word glossa means language; Luke uses it interchangeably with dialektos, dialect. Paul's rules in 1 Corinthians 14 are concrete: two or three speakers at most, each in turn, always interpreted, silence without an interpreter, the speaker in full control, everything done decently and in order, for God is not the author of confusion but of peace.

From Pentecost to Topeka: the history of glossolalia

If ecstatic tongues had been normal Christian devotion, history would show it. Instead the early church fathers fall silent on the practice; Chrysostom and Augustine say plainly the gift had ceased in their day; and the scattered exceptions (Montanists, Camisards, Shakers, Irvingites) were judged to be in error. The modern movement began on January 1, 1901 in Topeka, Kansas, when Agnes Ozman spoke in "tongues" at Charles Parham's Bethel Bible School. Claimed as missionary languages, the practice failed on the mission field and was redefined as a "heavenly language." Azusa Street (1906) carried it around the world; the Charismatic renewal (1960) and Third Wave (1980s) carried it into nearly every denomination; Toronto, Brownsville, and Lakeland followed. Linguists who analyzed recorded glossolalia, most extensively William Samarin, found it is not language.

Nothing in this book questions your sincerity, and nothing says what you felt was fake. The question it asks is gentler and more important: what was the source? Scripture invites every believer to test everything and hold fast what is good, and nothing real about God is ever lost by testing.
